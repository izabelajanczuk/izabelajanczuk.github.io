The Brief

Edmund Szyc Stadium is a fully abandoned, unsafe stadium at the center of Poznań, Poland — sitting on a low-lying wetland where the Warta River can rise a meter in a single day. This project asks what regenerative design could do with it: not a new stadium, but a park that honors the site's difficult history while giving the riverbed's wildlife room to return.

What
A regenerative park proposal for Poznań's abandoned Edmund Szyc Stadium.
Who
City residents and the Warta River's wetland wildlife.
Why
The site sits abandoned in the city center, on ecologically significant wetland terrain.
How
Combining historical commemoration, wetland-safe structures, and habitat design.

Background

Built in the early 20th century for the football club Warta Poznań, the stadium once held over 60,000 spectators — an enormous capacity for its time — and was named after Edmund Szyc, one of the club's founders.

Its history is a complicated one. Poor structural integrity, the Second World War, and decades of neglect led to its decline. During WWII the grounds were used as a forced labor camp for Poznań's Jewish population, and public executions were held there for those who attempted to escape. Postwar revival attempts by other local teams failed for lack of modernization, and today the stadium rests fully abandoned, with parts of it closed to the public over safety concerns.

"No." "I don't know." "I think it's just an abandoned stadium." "All I know is that the stadium had infrastructure issues." Only a couple of respondents could place it as the old Warta Poznań grounds — most Poznań residents pass the site without knowing what it once was.

Site & Ecology

The stadium sits on active wetland terrain, where the Warta River can rise up to a meter in a single day and four meters over the course of a year. Damp, moving ground like this already supports otters, beavers, storks, pike, and dragonflies, alongside stands of willow, reed, and yellow iris — the design had to work with that terrain, not against it.

Trees: willows, alder, poplars. Grasses: couch grass, meadow foxtail, purple loosestrife. Marsh: yellow iris, cattails. Water: common reed, water lilies, hornwort. Birds recorded nearby include the white stork, Eurasian bittern, common tern, marsh harrier, and bearded reedling.

Why Regenerative Design

For a site this culturally loaded, the project leans on regenerative design — an approach that restores a historically and culturally significant area without displacing it, focused equally on the community and on rehabilitating local wildlife. It's non-invasive and educational, creates a genuine win for both people and nature, fits the site's specific cultural and historical circumstances, makes good use of land that already exists, and addresses social, economic, and environmental problems all at once.

Four Focuses

Four goals shaped every design decision that followed.

I. Commemorating History

A meaningful memorial for the site's history — symbolic, easy to access, and non-invasive to the park around it.

II. Building on Wetland Terrain

Structures strong enough to withstand shifting water levels, erosion, and unstable ground — like boardwalks raised above the ecosystem rather than built into it.

III. Accommodating Wildlife

Bat boxes, nesting boxes, ponds, insect hotels, and local-only flora, with as little human interference and disturbance as possible.

IV. Incorporating Humans

Benches, informational plaques and maps, boardwalks, gravel pathways, a shelter, solar lighting, and a bike rack.

Precedent: Cytadela Park

Poznań's own Cytadela Park was the clearest local precedent — a former military site turned into a park that holds memorials, gardens, and everyday recreation all at once. Proof that a historically loaded site can carry all three without one crowding out the others.

Concept Development

Early sketches worked through how a memorial, a boardwalk, and a gathering space could sit inside the same wetland loop without disturbing it.

Outcome

The final proposal threads a boardwalk through the wetland, past a sunken amphitheater built into the old bleacher slope and a tunnel-turned-rest-stop, tying the park's memorial, ecological, and everyday functions into one walk.

To conclude this project, I believe that the Edmund Szyc Stadium is a perfect place to implement regenerative design. It is not only located in a wildlife hotspot, but it is also conveniently near the city center — meaning it has great potential for animal and human co-existence. Through holistic rehabilitation and by honoring the stadium's history, this area could become a much-needed green space in Poznań, for wildlife and people alike.
