SegoStop

Industrial Design // Urban Usability

Tools: Rhino, Laser Cutter, 3D Printer

An accessibility-focused modular seating system for Segovia's historic bus stops. Quarter-circle segments clamp around existing poles and adapt to uneven cobblestone terrain through independently adjustable legs — so everyone can sit.

In collaboration with Amina Magomedova, Ana Cotoi, Veer Chaudhary, Fergus Condon

← Back to projects

The Design Problem

Segovia's bus stop infrastructure lacks adjustable, accessible seating — especially for elderly residents. Harsh weather and uneven cobblestone terrain create additional barriers to usability, leaving many riders with no choice but to stand or sit on the ground.

Citizen Research — 14 Interviews
  • The lack of seating was a consistent annoyance, especially in newer parts of Segovia
  • A significant portion cited it as a problem for newer residents and tourists unfamiliar with bus schedules
  • 3 participants noted it affected them most when waiting without knowing arrival times
  • Results confirmed the problem was widespread enough to warrant a design intervention
Key Insights
  • 68% of surveyed users were over 60 years old
  • Key pain points: physical discomfort, exposure to weather, lack of stability on sloped ground
  • Existing solutions failed to adapt to Segovia's varied, uneven historic terrain

Target Users

Persona I — Maria

Age: 72

Gender: Female

Nationality: Spanish

Personality: Uses the bus 3–4 times weekly for errands and doctor visits. Has mild arthritis making long periods of standing painful. Concerned about slipping in rainy weather. Values predictability and reliability in public services.

Frustrations: The lack of bus stop seating makes commuting increasingly difficult at her age.

Persona II — Luis

Age: 65

Gender: Male

Nationality: Spanish

Personality: Recently retired teacher who uses public transit daily to visit grandchildren and the university. Has back pain that worsens with prolonged standing. Appreciates thoughtful urban design that respects all citizens.

Frustrations: Absence of comfortable seating makes it harder to manage his back pain on longer waits.

Ideation

Three prototype directions were developed, each with a different form, placement logic, and functionality range. Concepts were sketched and mapped on a shared flip chart, then evaluated against usability, durability, and the spatial constraints of real Segovia bus stop poles.

V1 — Foldable Chair Attachment

Pros: Foldable, compact, weather-resistant. Minimal footprint — does not require free space around the pole.

Cons: Seats at most two people (one on each side). Complex anti-theft attachment. Hard to maintain over time.

V2 — Pull Bench

Pros: Can accommodate more people than V1.

Cons: Easily destructible by weather and vandalism. Hard to maintain. Not fully resolved as a design — difficult to construct durably.

V3 — Modular "Doughnut" Segments (selected)

Pros: Uses the full footprint of the stop. Seats the most people. Easiest to maintain. Weather resistant. Modular — segments can be deployed partially where space is limited.

Cons: Larger overall footprint; not all poles have clear space around them. Contains multiple components.

A follow-up survey confirmed public preference: 8 out of 14 votes for the Doughnut design, versus 4 for the flip-chair and 2 for the extendable bench.

Sketching & Refinement

Sketches examined not just the final form but the methods of attachment to the bus pole and how seat segments might extend or retract. This allowed the team to eliminate ideas that seemed promising on paper but couldn't be realised — or didn't truly solve the problem.

The Terrain Problem

The original full-ring doughnut couldn't level itself on Segovia's uneven cobblestone terrain. A key pivot came when the team proposed independently adjustable legs for each segment — allowing every quarter-circle to find its own footing. This unlocked the final modular design.

3D Modeling

Before any physical prototype was built, each concept was modelled and rendered in Rhino. 3D modelling let the team identify structural flaws — such as complex attachment geometry or unclear movement of parts — that were invisible in two-dimensional sketches.

Physical Prototypes

Model 1 — Paper, 1:10 Scale

The first physical model established the overall doughnut form at a 1:10 scale — testing proportions and the ring's relationship to the pole before any material or segmentation decisions were made.

Model 2 — Cardboard, 1:5 Scale

The second iteration moved to 1:5 cardboard to explore segmentation — dividing the ring into four quarter-circle pieces and testing how they lock around the pole.

Scale Demonstration

Scale figures were placed on the assembled model to demonstrate seating capacity and communicate human proportion to reviewers.

Fabrication

Final-scale components were produced in the IE University FabLab. The steel-grey structural frame was 3D-printed from the Rhino model; the warm timber seat surface was laser-cut from wood veneer board. Combining both materials at true scale let the team test assembly logic, material feel, and the finished visual language.

Final Design

The final SegoStop is a modular, terrain-adaptive bench system for Segovia's existing bus stop network. Steel-framed quarter-circle segments — each with a warm timber slat seat surface — clamp around the existing sign pole. Independently adjustable legs allow each segment to level on any cobblestone or sloped surface. Any number of segments can be deployed around a single pole depending on available space.
