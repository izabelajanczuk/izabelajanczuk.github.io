01 — Research

The Design Problem

Segovia's bus stop infrastructure lacks adjustable, accessible seating — especially for elderly residents. Harsh weather and uneven cobblestone terrain create additional barriers, leaving many riders with no choice but to stand or sit on the ground.

14 Citizen interviews conducted
68% Of users surveyed were over 60

Key Pain Points

— Physical discomfort from prolonged standing, particularly affecting elderly residents with arthritis and back pain
— Exposure to weather with no shelter or support — especially problematic in rain when cobblestones become slippery
— Existing solutions fail to adapt to Segovia's varied, uneven historic terrain — a flat bench simply doesn't work on a slope

02 — Ideation

Three Directions

Three concept directions were developed — each with a different form, placement logic, and functionality. They were mapped on a shared flipchart and evaluated against usability, durability, and the spatial constraints of real Segovia bus stop poles.

V1

Foldable Chair Attachment

A compact seat that folds out from the pole. Minimal footprint — does not require clear space around the pole.

Pros: Foldable, compact, weather-resistant.

Cons: Seats at most two people. Complex anti-theft attachment. Hard to maintain.

V2

Pull Bench

An extendable bench that slides out from the pole base, allowing more people to sit side by side.

Pros: Can accommodate more people than V1.

Cons: Easily destructible by weather and vandalism. Difficult to construct durably.

Selected
V3

Modular “Doughnut” Segments

Quarter-circle segments that ring the pole, using its full footprint. Modular — can be partially deployed where space is limited.

Pros: Seats the most people. Easiest to maintain. Adapts to available space.

Cons: Larger footprint; not all poles have clear space around them.

Concept vote — 14 participants

V3 Doughnut
8 / 14
V1 Flip Chair
4 / 14
V2 Pull Bench
2 / 14

V3 received a clear majority — 8 of 14 votes — moving it forward as the direction to develop.

Design Pivot

“The problem wasn’t the ring. It was the ground.”

The original full-ring doughnut couldn’t level on Segovia’s uneven cobblestone. A rigid ring on an uneven surface rocks — or worse, tips. The solution: independently adjustable legs per segment, so each quarter-circle finds its own footing regardless of ground slope. The ring became four autonomous pieces that cooperate around the pole.

Original

Full rigid ring — works on flat ground

›

Problem

Rigid ring tilts on cobblestone

›

Solution

Four independent segments, each levels itself

03 — Making

Making Process

Five stages took the concept from pencil lines to a 3D-printed and laser-cut physical model alongside a fully rendered 3D model in Rhino.

01

Sketching

Sketches explored methods of attachment to the pole and how seat segments might extend or retract — letting the team eliminate ideas early that couldn’t be realised or didn’t truly solve the problem. The terrain pivot emerged here: fixed legs were replaced with independently adjustable ones.

02

Digital Model

“Rhino revealed what sketches could not: the geometry of the clamping ring and leg attachment had to be rethought entirely.”

Before any physical prototype, each concept was modelled in Rhino. 3D modelling revealed structural flaws — complex attachment geometry and unclear part movement — that were invisible in two-dimensional sketches. The digital model became the source for both laser-cut profiles and 3D-printed frame components.

03

Cardboard Prototyping

Scale: 1:10 → 1:5

Paper and cardboard models tested the ring-to-pole relationship and segment locking — first at 1:10, then 1:5. Cardboard let the team cut, adjust, and re-test in hours without committing to any material.

1:5 scale — four quarter-circle segments testing pole attachment and the ring’s overall proportions before committing to final materials.

04

Fabrication

The structural frame was 3D-printed directly from the Rhino model. The seat slats were laser-cut from wood veneer board. Combining both materials for the first time tested assembly logic, feel, and the finished visual language.

Produced at the IE University FabLab — frame and seat slats assembled at final scale for the first time.

05

Scaled Model

Scale figures placed on the assembled model confirmed seating capacity, human proportion, and sight lines — communicating the design’s usability to reviewers without a full-size prototype. The model demonstrated how three of the four segments could be deployed around a single pole.

04 — Final Design

SegoStop

The final SegoStop is a modular, terrain-adaptive seating system for Segovia’s existing bus stop network. Quarter-circle segments clamp around the existing sign pole — each with independently adjustable legs that level the seat on any cobblestone or sloped surface.

Any number of segments can be deployed around a single pole depending on available space. The system requires no ground fixings and no changes to existing infrastructure.

Design Features

Quarter-circle modularity

Each segment is independent — one, two, three, or four can be placed around any pole depending on available pavement space.

Adjustable legs

Four independently height-adjustable legs per segment let each piece find level on sloped or uneven cobblestone without shimming.

Pole-clamp attachment

Segments clamp to the existing sign pole — no drilling, no ground fixings, no infrastructure changes required.

Radiating wood seat slats

Laser-cut wood veneer slats fan outward from the pole centre, referencing Segovia’s craft heritage while keeping weight low.

Accessible ergonomics

Seat height and depth sized for comfortable use by elderly users — the 68% majority of the surveyed waiting population.
