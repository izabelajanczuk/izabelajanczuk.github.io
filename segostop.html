SegoStop

Industrial Design // Urban Usability

Tools: Rhino, Laser Cutter, 3D Printer

An accessibility-focused modular seating system for Segovia's historic bus stops. Quarter-circle segments clamp around existing poles and adapt to uneven cobblestone terrain through independently adjustable legs — so everyone can sit.

In collaboration with Amina Magomedova, Ana Cotoi, Veer Chaudhary, Fergus Condon

← Back to projects

The Design Problem

Segovia's bus stop infrastructure lacks adjustable, accessible seating — especially for elderly residents. Harsh weather and uneven cobblestone terrain create additional barriers, leaving many riders with no choice but to stand or sit on the ground.

Citizen Research — 14 Interviews
  • The lack of seating was a consistent annoyance, especially in newer parts of Segovia
  • A significant portion cited it as a particular problem for newer residents and tourists unfamiliar with schedules
  • 3 participants noted it affected them most when waiting without knowing arrival times
  • Results confirmed the problem was widespread enough to warrant a design intervention
Key Insights
  • 68% of surveyed users were over 60 years old
  • Key pain points: physical discomfort, exposure to weather, lack of stability on sloped ground
  • Existing solutions failed to adapt to Segovia's varied, uneven historic terrain

Target Users

Persona I — Maria

Age: 72

Gender: Female

Nationality: Spanish

Personality: Uses the bus 3–4 times weekly for errands and doctor visits. Has mild arthritis making long periods of standing painful. Concerned about slipping in rainy weather.

Frustrations: The lack of bus stop seating makes commuting increasingly difficult at her age.

Persona II — Luis

Age: 65

Gender: Male

Nationality: Spanish

Personality: Recently retired teacher who uses public transit daily. Has back pain that worsens with prolonged standing. Appreciates thoughtful urban design that respects all citizens.

Frustrations: Absence of comfortable seating makes it harder to manage his back pain on longer waits.

Ideation

Three directions were developed — each with a different form, placement logic, and functionality. Concepts were mapped on a shared flipchart and evaluated against usability, durability, and the spatial constraints of real Segovia bus stop poles.

V1 — Foldable Chair Attachment

Pros: Foldable, compact, weather-resistant. Minimal footprint — does not require clear space around the pole.

Cons: Seats at most two people. Complex anti-theft attachment. Hard to maintain.

V2 — Pull Bench

Pros: Can accommodate more people than V1.

Cons: Easily destructible by weather and vandalism. Difficult to construct durably.

V3 — Modular "Doughnut" Segments (selected)

Pros: Uses the full footprint of the stop. Seats the most people. Easiest to maintain. Modular — can be partially deployed where space is limited.

Cons: Larger footprint; not all poles have clear space around them.

Follow-up survey: 8 out of 14 votes for the Doughnut, versus 4 for the flip-chair and 2 for the extendable bench.

1
Sketching & Refinement

Sketches explored methods of attachment to the pole and how seat segments might extend or retract — letting the team eliminate ideas early that couldn't be realised or didn't truly solve the problem.

Terrain pivot — The original full-ring doughnut couldn't level on Segovia's uneven cobblestone. Solution: independently adjustable legs per segment, so each quarter-circle finds its own footing regardless of ground slope.

3D Modeling

Before any physical prototype, each concept was modelled in Rhino. 3D modelling revealed structural flaws — complex attachment geometry and unclear part movement — that were invisible in two-dimensional sketches.

2
Cardboard Prototyping

Paper and cardboard models tested the ring-to-pole relationship and segment locking — first at 1:10, then 1:5. Cardboard let the team cut, adjust, and re-test in hours without committing to any material.

1:5 scale — four quarter-circle segments testing pole attachment and the ring's overall proportions before committing to final materials.
3
Laser Cutting & 3D Printing

The structural frame was 3D-printed directly from the Rhino model. The seat slats were laser-cut from wood veneer board. Combining both materials for the first time tested assembly logic, feel, and the finished visual language.

Produced at the IE University FabLab — frame and seat slats assembled at final scale for the first time.
4
Scaled Model

Scale figures placed on the assembled model confirmed seating capacity, human proportion, and sight lines — communicating the design's usability to reviewers without a full-size prototype.

Final Design

The final SegoStop is a modular, terrain-adaptive seating system for Segovia's existing bus stop network. Quarter-circle segments clamp around the existing sign pole — each with independently adjustable legs that level the seat on any cobblestone or sloped surface. Any number of segments can be deployed around a single pole depending on available space.
